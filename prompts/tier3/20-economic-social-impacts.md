# Economic and Social Impacts — Tier 3

You are a knowledgeable ML instructor. Teach me **Economic and Social Impacts of AI at research depth** in a focused, interactive session at the Tier 3 level — this is research-depth material that goes beyond the standard practitioner knowledge covered in Tiers 1 and 2.

## What to assume about my background

I have completed all Tier 1 and Tier 2 sessions from this reading list. That means I understand:
- Neural networks, backpropagation, and gradient descent in detail
- Full transformer architecture (multi-head attention, positional encoding, layer norm, residual stream)
- Tokenization and BPE
- Major foundation models: GPT-2/3, LLaMA, InstructGPT, DeepSeek-R1/V3, Llama 3
- RLHF, DPO, GRPO, and process reward models
- Chain-of-thought, self-consistency, and test-time compute scaling
- Task decomposition, Tree of Thoughts, IDA, factored cognition
- Debate as a scalable oversight technique
- RAG, dense retrieval, and semantic embeddings
- Calibration, uncertainty estimation, and sparse autoencoders
- Mechanistic interpretability basics (activation patching, monosemanticity)
- AlphaZero, MuZero, and the connection between game RL and LLM post-training
- Scaling laws, Chinchilla, and the AI compute trajectory
- AI safety framing: misuse, misalignment, structural risks, inner/outer alignment

I am familiar with the general argument that AI could automate cognitive labor and have large economic effects. I understand the concept of automation bias and labor displacement from historical industrial automation. I have not studied the specific formal economic models of AI-driven growth or the empirical literature on information asymmetry in markets in depth.

## Session focus

Tier 3 examines two substantive research contributions on economic impact: the formal economic argument for why AI automation of cognitive labor could produce discontinuous (explosive) growth rather than gradual acceleration — and where that argument is contested — and an empirical study on how LLMs affect information asymmetry in markets, with implications for equity and access.

## Resources for this session

- "Explosive growth from AI automation: A review of the arguments" (Erdil & Besiroglu, 2023)
- "Language Models Can Reduce Asymmetry in Information Markets" (Hu et al., 2024)

## Teaching objectives

By the end of this session I should be able to:
- Explain the explosive growth argument: the theoretical conditions under which automating cognitive labor (especially AI R&D itself) could lead to self-accelerating economic growth — the feedback loop structure, what "explosive" means quantitatively (GDP growth rates far above historical norms), and how this differs from gradual acceleration
- Explain the key assumptions the explosive growth argument requires: that AI can substitute for human cognitive labor broadly, that deployment can scale fast enough, that there are no hard bottlenecks (energy, physical infrastructure, regulatory) that cap the growth rate — and evaluate which of these assumptions are most contested in the economics literature
- Explain the strongest counterarguments: economic bottlenecks to growth that might prevent discontinuity even with very capable AI (Baumol's cost disease applied to non-automatable sectors, physical deployment constraints, coordination costs, diminishing returns to scale in key inputs)
- Explain the information asymmetry result: the theoretical and empirical case for how LLMs democratize access to expert knowledge (legal, medical, financial), what kinds of information asymmetries are reduced vs. which persist, and what the measured economic effects on users were
- Discuss the distribution question with specificity: who benefits from cheaper expertise (access equity), who is displaced (labor market effects on knowledge workers), and what the net welfare picture looks like given current evidence
- Synthesize: given Erdil & Besiroglu and the information asymmetry work together, articulate a nuanced view of the likely economic trajectory — not naive optimism or pessimism, but the actual range of outcomes the evidence supports

## How to run this session

1. Start with "here's what you know from Tiers 1 and 2 — here's what Tier 3 adds" framing.
2. Focus on the *surprising*, *counterintuitive*, or *contested* findings — that's what Tier 3 is for.
3. Flag when a result is actively debated in the field or when the evidence is mixed.
4. At the end, give me a 3-question quiz that tests Tier 3 understanding specifically.

Stay focused on this topic at the Tier 3 level. Tier 4 material can be noted briefly but not taught here.
